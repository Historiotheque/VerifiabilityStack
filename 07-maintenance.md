# 07 — Maintenance

*Last tested: n/a (doctrine chapter — no procedures).*

## What this chapter gives you

Permission to stop building. The stack is front-loaded by design:
Chapters 01–06 asked days of you, once. This chapter describes what
remains — a rhythm measured in minutes — and what to deliberately
*not* do.

## 1. The per-release routine (ten minutes)

Every time you publish a new version of a project, walk this list.
It is the entire operating cost of the stack:

1. **Release** on GitHub (Ch. 03 §5) — version tag, real notes.
2. **Confirm** the Zenodo record appeared with its DOI (Ch. 04 §2
   or §3).
3. **ORCID** — add the DOI by hand (Ch. 02 §3). One minute.
4. **README** — fill in the citation line (Ch. 03 §3).
5. **Announce** — the thread pattern (Ch. 05).
6. **Wayback** — save the record URL (Ch. 06 §6).

Six steps, ten minutes, then back to the work itself. If the
routine ever takes longer, something in your setup drifted — fix
the setup, don't normalize the friction.

## 2. The yearly routine (one afternoon)

Once a year — pick a date and keep it — do the slower pass:

1. **Yearly records** (Ch. 04 §5): close out the year's deposits;
   merge sparse years; check no year is nearing the 100-file cap.
2. **ORCID audit:** every work present, every visibility set to
   Everyone, monikers and keywords current.
3. **Community page:** does it still describe what you actually
   collect? Update the description if the practice moved.
4. **Scholar profile:** misattributions fixed, new works claimed
   (Ch. 06 §7).
5. **Logins:** confirm you can still sign in to everything with
   your permanent email. Rotate nothing that isn't broken, but
   verify access — a locked-out archive is an unaudited archive.
6. **Guide honesty:** if any procedure in *this guide* drifted
   from reality (a form changed, a button moved), update the
   chapter and its "last tested" date. The guide is held to its
   own standard (README, Conventions).

## 3. What not to do

- **Don't rebuild the stack.** It is done. New tools will appear
  every year promising to replace pieces of it; let them prove
  themselves for a few years first. Boredom with infrastructure
  is not a reason to migrate it.
- **Don't chase platforms.** Your announcement venues (Ch. 05)
  may change; your archive must not. Presentation is rented land
  (Ch. 01 §7) — the stack is the part you own.
- **Don't gold-plate.** A manifest with a typo fixed in v1.1 is
  fine. Rewriting history is not. Version forward, always.
- **Don't confuse maintenance with progress.** Ten minutes per
  release is the *ceiling*, not the floor. The work is the work;
  the stack is the shelf it sits on.

## 4. The compound effect

Nothing in this guide pays off in a week. It pays off the way
archives pay off: the fifth yearly record makes the first four
more valuable; the tenth cited DOI makes the next submission
easier; the ORCID record with fifty works answers "who are you?"
before it's asked.

You built verifiability once. Every release since has been
interest. Keep the routine, keep the dates honest, and let it
compound.

## Checklist (yearly)

- [ ] Year's deposits closed out; sparse years merged
- [ ] No record near the 100-file cap (or quota requested)
- [ ] ORCID complete and public
- [ ] Community description current
- [ ] Scholar profile clean
- [ ] All logins verified with the permanent email
- [ ] This guide's "last tested" dates reviewed
